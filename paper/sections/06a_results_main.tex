\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Learning dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows greedy validation performance during training, averaged over ten seeds. AA-DQN improves rapidly during the first 1000 episodes and slowly thereafter; it is still improving at the end of the 5000-episode budget. The seed-averaged curve of the \emph{current} parameters remains below the best tuned heuristic (SPT-Threshold) throughout training, in both return and on-time rate. This average, however, hides substantial variation between checkpoints within each run: the best checkpoints of every seed reach the heuristic's level, while neighbouring checkpoints fall below it. Such policy oscillation is typical of value-based deep RL with a constant learning rate \cite{henderson2018}, and it motivates selecting checkpoints on held-out validation days. Validation-selected checkpoints were taken at episode \AADQNBestEp{} on average for AA-DQN and \DQNBestEp{} for DQN. Because validation and test days are disjoint, the test results below are not inflated by this selection. The vanilla DQN learns more slowly and plateaus well below AA-DQN. Its training return is also less stable (Supplementary Fig.~S2), consistent with the over-estimation that Double Q-learning mitigates \cite{vanhasselt2016} and with the known weakness of memoryless policies in POMDPs \cite{singh1994}.

\subsection{Comparison with baselines}\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figs.~\ref{fig:main}--\ref{fig:tradeoff} report performance on the 500 held-out test days, and Table~\ref{tab:tests} gives the paired statistical comparisons. Four findings stand out.

\emph{(i) Capacity-agnostic rules fail under cognitive dynamics.} The rules that never rest (FIFO, EDF, SPT) drive the worker into a low-capacity regime: mean fatigue reaches 0.48--0.55, attention falls to 0.46--0.54, and EDF completes only \EDFOnTimePct\% of tasks on time. EDF performs particularly poorly, which reproduces the overload domino effect known from real-time scheduling \cite{buttazzo2011}: once capacity falls, chasing the most urgent task means working on tasks that will be late anyway. SPT limits the damage by finishing short tasks first (\SPTOnTimePct\%).

\emph{(ii) State-aware rest is the dominant lever.} Adding rest to these rules, either periodically or when observed fatigue or attention crosses a tuned threshold, raises the return by 4--8 units and roughly halves mean fatigue. All competitive policies rest in 15--20\% of slots. The best rule overall is \bestHeur{} (return \bestHeurReturn, on-time rate \bestHeurOnTimePct\%), which combines SPT sequencing with threshold-triggered rest.

\emph{(iii) AA-DQN matches the best tuned rule on return and is the only policy that is strong on both completion measures.} AA-DQN achieves a return of \aaReturn{}~$\pm$~\aaReturnSD{} across seeds. This is statistically indistinguishable from \bestHeur{} ($\Delta=\dRetSPTThreshold$, 95\% CI [\dRetSPTThresholdLo, \dRetSPTThresholdHi], $\pRetSPTThreshold$), and it significantly exceeds every other baseline ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-4}$). It attains the highest on-time rate of all methods (\aaOnTimePct\%), significantly above \bestHeur{} ($+\dOnSPTThreshold$ percentage points [\dOnSPTThresholdLo, \dOnSPTThresholdHi], $\pOnSPTThreshold$, all ten seeds better). The difficulty-weighted on-time rate reveals why the two leading families differ (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). SPT-based rules complete many tasks on time by finishing short tasks first, but they neglect hard tasks (weighted on-time rate \SPTThresholdWOnTimePct\%). EDF-based rules protect hard tasks (\EDFPeriodicWOnTimePct\%) but complete fewer tasks overall. AA-DQN combines the strengths of both: it completes as many tasks on time as any rule, while matching the EDF rules on difficulty-weighted completion (\AADQNWOnTimePct\%; $+\dWOnSPTThreshold$ pp over \bestHeur{}, [\dWOnSPTThresholdLo, \dWOnSPTThresholdHi]). In other words, the learned policy avoids the starvation of long tasks that is the classical weakness of SPT \cite{pinedo2016}. No single hand-designed rule in our set achieves this.

\emph{(iv) The learned policy pays for this with somewhat more fatigue and fewer late completions.} AA-DQN rests less than the tuned heuristics (\AADQNBreakPct\% versus \bestHeurBreakPct\% of slots for \bestHeur{}). Its mean fatigue is therefore higher (\aaFatigue{} versus \bestHeurFatigue), although still \fatigueRedVsEdfPct\% below EDF, and fatigue never exceeds 0.7. Because it focuses effort on tasks that can still meet their deadline, it also completes fewer tasks eventually (\AADQNComplPct\% versus \SPTThresholdComplPct\% for \bestHeur{}, counting late completions). This explains why its return, which rewards late completions and low fatigue, is not higher despite the higher on-time rate. Section~\ref{sec:pareto} shows that the fatigue level can be shifted by the reward weight.

Relative to the memoryless vanilla DQN, adding memory, Double Q-learning and the dueling head increases the return by \dRetVsDqn{} ([\dRetVsDqnLo, \dRetVsDqnHi]), with a win on \winRetVsDqnPct\% of test days. The vanilla DQN would rank only seventh among the eleven methods, which shows that a naive application of DQN to this POMDP would wrongly suggest that learning is not competitive. Validation-based checkpoint selection matters for both agents (Supplementary Table~S3): the final-iterate AA-DQN has a return of \AADQNFinalReturn{} and an on-time rate of \AADQNFinalOnTimePct\%, and the final-iterate DQN has a return of \DQNFinalReturn. We report the validation-selected policies as primary results because the heuristics received an equivalent selection step (grid search) on their own held-out days.
