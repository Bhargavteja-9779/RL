\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Learning dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows greedy validation performance during training, averaged over ten seeds. AA-DQN improves rapidly during the first 1000 episodes and slowly thereafter. The seed-averaged curve of its \emph{current} parameters remains below the best tuned rule (SPT-Threshold) throughout training. This average hides substantial variation between checkpoints within each run: the best checkpoints of every seed reach the rule's level, while neighbouring checkpoints fall below it. Such policy oscillation is typical of value-based deep RL with a constant learning rate \cite{henderson2018}, and it motivates selecting checkpoints on held-out validation days. \rev{AA-PPO learns more slowly at first, overtakes AA-DQN after about 1700 episodes, exceeds the best rule on validation days after about 3000 episodes and is still improving at the end of the budget. Its learning curve is smooth: the final iterate is almost as good as the validation-selected checkpoint (return \PPOFinalReturn{} versus \PPOSelReturn), whereas for AA-DQN checkpoint selection matters (\AADQNFinalReturn{} versus \AADQNSelReturn; Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:s-ckpt}).}\revnote{R4.4} Validation-selected checkpoints were taken at episode \AADQNBestEp{} on average for AA-DQN, \PPOBestEp{} for AA-PPO and \DQNBestEp{} for DQN. Because validation and test days are disjoint, the test results below are not inflated by this selection. The vanilla DQN learns more slowly and plateaus well below AA-DQN, consistent with the over-estimation that Double Q-learning mitigates \cite{vanhasselt2016} and with the known weakness of memoryless policies in POMDPs \cite{singh1994}.

\subsection{Comparison with baselines}\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figs.~\ref{fig:main}--\ref{fig:tradeoff} report performance on the 500 held-out test days. Table~\ref{tab:tests} gives the paired statistical comparisons for AA-PPO with the hybrid action set; the corresponding comparisons for AA-DQN are given in Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:s-tests}. Four findings stand out.

\emph{(i) Capacity-agnostic rules fail under cognitive dynamics.} The rules that never rest (FIFO, EDF, SPT) drive the worker into a low-capacity regime: mean fatigue reaches 0.48--0.55, attention falls to 0.46--0.54, and EDF completes only \EDFOnTimePct\% of tasks on time. EDF performs particularly poorly, which reproduces the overload domino effect known from real-time scheduling \cite{buttazzo2011}: once capacity falls, chasing the most urgent task means working on tasks that will be late anyway. SPT limits the damage by finishing short tasks first (\SPTOnTimePct\%).

\emph{(ii) State-aware rest is the dominant lever.} Adding rest to these rules, either periodically or when observed fatigue or attention crosses a tuned threshold, raises the return by 4--8 units and roughly halves mean fatigue. All competitive policies rest in 15--20\% of slots. The best rule overall is \bestHeur{} (return \bestHeurReturn, on-time rate \bestHeurOnTimePct\%), which combines SPT sequencing with threshold-triggered rest.

\rev{\emph{(iii) The learned policies match or exceed the best tuned rule, and AA-PPO exceeds it on every task-based metric.}\revnote{R4.4; R4.5; R4.6} AA-PPO achieves a return of \PPOReturn{}~$\pm$~\PPOReturnSD{} across seeds and an on-time rate of \PPOOnTimePct\%. Relative to \bestHeur, the paired differences are $+\PpoRetSPTThreshold$ in return (95\% CI [\PpoRetSPTThresholdLo, \PpoRetSPTThresholdHi], $\pPpoRetSPTThreshold$), $+\PpoOnSPTThreshold$ percentage points in on-time rate ([\PpoOnSPTThresholdLo, \PpoOnSPTThresholdHi]) and $+\PpoWOnSPTThreshold$ pp in difficulty-weighted on-time rate ([\PpoWOnSPTThresholdLo, \PpoWOnSPTThresholdHi]), and all ten seeds are better than the rule on each of these metrics. AA-PPO also significantly exceeds every other rule in return and on-time rate ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-4}$), and it exceeds AA-DQN by \PpoRetAADQN{} in return. With the intensity-only action set, AA-PPO is slightly better still (return \PPOThreeactReturn, on-time rate \PPOThreeactOnTimePct\%; $+\PpoIRetSPTThreshold$ [\PpoIRetSPTThresholdLo, \PpoIRetSPTThresholdHi] in return over \bestHeur) and has the smallest seed-to-seed variability of all learners (s.d.\ \PPOThreeactReturnSD). AA-DQN with the hybrid action set ties with \bestHeur{} in return ($\Delta=\dRetSPTThreshold$, [\dRetSPTThresholdLo, \dRetSPTThresholdHi], $\pRetSPTThreshold$) but completes more tasks on time (\aaOnTimePct\%; $+\dOnSPTThreshold$ pp, $\pOnSPTThreshold$).}

The difficulty-weighted on-time rate reveals why the learned policies are better (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). SPT-based rules complete many tasks on time by finishing short tasks first, but they neglect hard tasks (weighted on-time rate \SPTThresholdWOnTimePct\%). EDF-based rules protect hard tasks (\EDFPeriodicWOnTimePct\%) but complete fewer tasks overall. The learned policies combine the strengths of both. AA-PPO completes more tasks on time than any rule, while matching the EDF rules on difficulty-weighted completion (\PPOWOnTimePct\%; the difference from EDF-Periodic, $+\PpoWOnEDFPeriodic$ pp, is not significant, $\pPpoWOnEDFPeriodic$). In other words, the learned policies avoid the starvation of long tasks that is the classical weakness of SPT \cite{pinedo2016}. No single hand-designed rule in our set achieves this.

\emph{(iv) The gains are not bought with lower completion, but the learned policies keep the worker somewhat more fatigued than the best rule.} AA-PPO completes almost as many tasks in total as \bestHeur{} (\PPOComplPct\% versus \SPTThresholdComplPct\%, counting late completions), so its higher on-time rate does not come from abandoning tasks. It rests in \PPOBreakPct\% of slots versus \bestHeurBreakPct\% for \bestHeur, and its mean fatigue is higher by \PpoFatSPTThreshold{} (\PPOFatigue{} versus \bestHeurFatigue). This is still 46\% below EDF, and the mean fatigue stays below 0.5 in every slot of the day (Fig.~\ref{fig:behaviour}). AA-DQN rests least among the attention-aware learners (\AADQNBreakPct\%) and has the highest fatigue of the learned policies (\aaFatigue). Section~\ref{sec:pareto} shows that the fatigue level can be lowered through the reward weight without losing on-time performance.

Relative to the memoryless vanilla DQN, adding memory, Double Q-learning and the dueling head increases the return by \dRetVsDqn{} ([\dRetVsDqnLo, \dRetVsDqnHi]), with a win on \winRetVsDqnPct\% of test days. The vanilla DQN ranks below five of the nine rules, so a naive application of DQN to this POMDP would wrongly suggest that learning is not competitive. We report the validation-selected policies as primary results because the rules received an equivalent selection step (grid search) on their own held-out days; the final-iterate results lead to the same ranking among the learners (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:s-ckpt}).
