\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper formulated attention-aware task scheduling as a partially observable Markov decision process. In this formulation a worker's attention, fatigue and circadian state shape their capacity, rest is an explicit action, and the cognitive state is observed only through noisy estimates. We proposed a memory-augmented deep reinforcement learning framework, instantiated it with a value-based learner (AA-DQN) and a policy-gradient learner (AA-PPO), and evaluated both against nine rule-based schedulers, including grid-tuned EDF and SPT rules with periodic or state-triggered rest. The evaluation used ten training seeds, 500 held-out workdays with common random numbers, reward-independent metrics and multiplicity-corrected tests.

Rules that ignore cognitive dynamics performed poorly, and every competitive policy rested in 15--20\% of the day. Well-tuned rest rules were strong competitors: AA-DQN matched the return of the best of them and completed more tasks on time. AA-PPO exceeded the best rule in return (+\PpoRetSPTThreshold), on-time rate (\PPOOnTimePct\% versus \bestHeurOnTimePct\%) and difficulty-weighted on-time rate (+\PpoWOnSPTThreshold{} percentage points), with every seed better than the rule. The learned policies are the only ones that combine high task throughput with protection of demanding tasks. They do hard work while the worker is fresh, fill the afternoon trough with easy tasks and rest before and during the post-lunch dip. AA-PPO remained the best method under sensor noise, for resilient and fatigue-prone workers, for heavier workloads and under $\pm30\%$ changes of every cognitive parameter, whereas AA-DQN generalised poorly to unseen workloads. Controlled ablations showed that, within this simulator, modelling cognition is neither necessary nor sufficient for learning to help; the value of learning grows mainly with the reliability of the cognitive-state estimate, memory is the critical architectural component, and a moderate weight on fatigue improves both well-being and productivity.

The study is based on a literature-grounded but uncalibrated simulator. Its main limitation is therefore the gap to real workers, which future work should close through calibration with physiological and behavioural data, personalised policies and user studies. The released simulator, agents and evaluation protocol provide a reproducible basis for that work and for fair comparisons of learned and hand-designed schedulers in human-centric systems.
