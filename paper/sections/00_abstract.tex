\begin{abstract}
Classical dispatching rules such as first-in-first-out and earliest-deadline-first assume that the resource executing work has constant capacity, an assumption that fails when the resource is a person whose attention is depleted by sustained effort, whose fatigue accumulates, and whose alertness follows a circadian rhythm. This paper formulates human-centric task scheduling as a partially observable Markov decision process. Rest is an explicit action, the worker's attention and fatigue are latent states observed only through noisy estimates, and a multi-objective reward balances deadline-weighted task value against cognitive load. We propose an attention-aware deep Q-network (AA-DQN) that combines a short observation history, which approximates the belief state, with Double Q-learning and a dueling architecture. In a cognitively grounded simulator with stochastic task arrivals, AA-DQN was evaluated against seven baselines, including grid-tuned heuristics that rest according to observed fatigue and attention, using ten training seeds, 500 held-out workdays with common random numbers, and multiplicity-corrected paired tests. AA-DQN completed \aaOnTimePct\% of tasks on time, compared with \bestHeurOnTimePct\% for the best tuned heuristic, \edfOnTimePct\% for earliest-deadline-first and \DQNOnTimePct\% for a memoryless DQN, while reducing mean fatigue by \fatigueRedVsEdfPct\% relative to earliest-deadline-first (all $p<10^{-4}$). The learned policy works intensively while the worker is fresh, concentrates breaks around the post-lunch circadian dip, and schedules demanding tasks when attention is high. Ablations show that the advantage of learning depends on modelling dynamic cognition and grows with the reliability of state estimates, and zero-shot tests quantify robustness to sensor noise, worker heterogeneity and workload.
\end{abstract}
